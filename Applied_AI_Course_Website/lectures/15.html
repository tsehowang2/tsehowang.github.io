Generation means modeling possible outputs

Our text model generates a sequence by repeatedly predicting a token. Generative AI also includes images, sound, and other outputs. The common idea is learning a distribution or generation process; the mechanisms need not all be next-word prediction.

Imagine a collection of handwritten pictures of buses. A classifier might predict whether an image contains a bus. A generator aims to produce new samples with relevant structure.

A generated image can look plausible without reproducing a particular training image. It can also contain errors. As with language, plausibility and task correctness need separate evaluation.

A latent representation and a decoder

An autoencoder maps an input into a compressed representation and reconstructs it. A variational autoencoder adds a probabilistic latent formulation and regularization that encourages a useful latent distribution.

Think of a small vector controlling several aspects of a drawing, but do not assume each coordinate automatically has a clean human label. The network learns a representation under its objective.

A basic VAE objective balances reconstruction with a term relating an approximate latent posterior to a chosen prior. The model can then sample a latent and decode it. A bottleneck picture explains the roles before the full variational derivation.

Adversarial learning

A GAN trains a generator against a discriminator. The discriminator learns to distinguish relevant training examples from generated examples under the training setup; the generator tries to produce outputs that challenge it.

The two objectives interact. A strong-looking sample alone does not show that the learned distribution covers all important cases. Training instability and limited diversity are issues to investigate.

We study the arrangement to distinguish it from token likelihood training, not to build a production image generator during this course.

Diffusion starts from corruption

Take a simple eight-by-eight pattern representing a letter. Add controlled Gaussian noise to make increasingly corrupted versions. A diffusion model learns a denoising-related prediction across noise levels.

The forward formula can be written x_t=√ᾱ_t x_0+√(1−ᾱ_t) ε, where ε is standard Gaussian noise. The coefficients determine the relative signal and noise.

At ᾱ=1, the sample is the original signal. At ᾱ=0, the formula gives noise. Intermediate values are mixtures. A slider illustrating this forward formula does not itself implement a learned reverse model.

A concrete scalar diffusion calculation

Let x0=2, ε=-1, and ᾱ=0.64. Then √ᾱ=0.8 and √(1−ᾱ)=0.6, giving x_t=0.8×2+0.6×(-1)=1.

This arithmetic is the same for each element of a simple tensor under the noising expression. A learned model predicts noise or a related target, and sampling uses a reverse procedure with a schedule.

Moving a display slider backward can reveal less noisy versions because the original signal remains known to the visualization. That is different from learning to generate a new sample starting from unknown noise.

What the denoiser learns and how generation runs backward

During training we know a clean example x_0. Sample a noise level t and Gaussian noise ε, construct x_t with the forward formula, and ask a network ε_θ(x_t,t) to predict that noise. A common simplified loss is the mean squared error between ε and ε_θ. For the scalar example above, if the actual ε is −1 and the prediction is −0.8, squared error is 0.04. The network must learn across many clean examples and noise levels.

Generation starts with a new noise sample, not the original image. At each scheduled step, the denoiser predicts noise or another trained target, and a sampler uses that prediction to form a less noisy state. In the DDPM parameterization, its reverse mean is μ_θ(x_t,t)=(x_t−β_t ε_θ(x_t,t)/√(1−ᾱ_t))/√α_t, where α_t=1−β_t and ᾱ_t is the accumulated product. A reverse step may add scheduled random noise as well. This is a learned transition; sliding backward through stored corruptions is only a visualization.

Latent diffusion and DiT move the same idea to different representations

Latent diffusion first uses an image autoencoder to represent a clean image by a smaller latent tensor. Diffusion training and sampling operate in that latent space; an image decoder converts the final latent back to pixels. This reduces the spatial size of repeated denoising work while making the autoencoder’s reconstruction quality part of the system.

A Diffusion Transformer, or DiT, uses transformer blocks as the denoising backbone, often on patches of image latents. Image-patch tokens carry spatial information and noise-level conditioning rather than ordinary word meanings. Replacing a U-Net with a transformer changes the predictor architecture; it does not turn diffusion into left-to-right word prediction.

Classifier-free guidance combines conditional and unconditional predictions

For a prompt such as “a red school bus”, train a conditional denoiser while sometimes dropping the condition. At sampling, obtain predictions ε_cond with the prompt and ε_uncond without it. One common convention combines them as ε_guided=ε_uncond+w(ε_cond−ε_uncond). Here w=1 gives the conditional prediction and w>1 strengthens its difference from the unconditional prediction. If ε_uncond=0.2, ε_cond=0.5, and w=2, the combined scalar prediction is 0.8. Larger guidance can improve adherence while changing diversity or producing artifacts; report the convention because APIs may parameterize guidance differently.

Flow matching learns a direction of transport

A simple flow-matching example joins a noise point x_noise=−1 to a data point x_data=2 by x_t=(1−t)x_noise+t*x_data. The target velocity along this chosen straight path is 3. At t=0.4 the point is 0.2. A neural network learns a velocity field from many such training paths; an ODE solver follows the learned field from noise toward data. Actual model designs choose paths and parameterizations carefully. This example distinguishes a learned transport direction from DDPM’s noise-prediction target.

A vision-language model reads an image and produces language

A common VLM connects an image encoder, a projection or connector, and a language model. For a school-bus photograph, the image encoder produces visual features; the connector maps them into representations the language model can use alongside text tokens. The model then generates an answer to “What color is the bus?” Training may align image-text pairs and later use image-conditioned instructions. A VLM can still misread small text, count incorrectly, or infer unseen details, so evaluate visual evidence and answer accuracy separately.

Flow ideas and multimodal tokens

Normalizing flows use invertible transformations with a tractable change-of-variables formulation. Flow matching learns a vector field transporting between distributions. They are related to generative modeling but should not be treated as synonyms for every diffusion implementation.

Some multimodal systems represent images or audio using patches, features, or discrete tokens and connect them to transformer components. The representation and objective define what the model predicts.

A language-model vocabulary and an image-patch representation have different semantics. “The model uses tokens” does not mean the tokens correspond to ordinary words.

Comparing families by the question each answers

For autoregressive text, ask which next-token conditional is modeled and how generation proceeds. For a VAE, ask about latent variables, reconstruction, and prior matching. For a GAN, ask about the generator-discriminator training game. For diffusion, ask about forward corruption and the learned reverse-related predictions.

These questions are more informative than memorizing a list of names. A system may combine components from several families.

Keep the scope clear: our deep implementation path remains the text transformer. The other families receive conceptual and numerical introductions with optional deeper readings.

Worked practice and a readiness check

Calculate the forward-noising formula at ᾱ=0 and ᾱ=1 for the scalar example. Then explain why an image classifier and image generator have different output tasks.

Draw the roles in a VAE and a GAN without claiming that every latent coordinate has a fixed interpretation. Write one sentence describing what diffusion learns beyond displaying noisy pictures.

You are ready when generative AI is broader than LLMs in your mental model and you can compare mechanisms without collapsing them into a single buzzword.

Different kinds of uncertainty

A text prefix may have several sensible next words. An image prompt may admit many valid pictures. A spoken sentence may be delivered with different voices and timing. Generative modeling must accommodate variation rather than assume there is always one exact output.

Evaluation therefore depends on the task. A bus fare calculation has a checkable numerical answer. A new bus illustration has many acceptable appearances, but it can still fail an instruction such as “show exactly two wheels.” A sample can be diverse yet wrong, or accurate to a narrow instruction yet repetitive across a collection.

This helps explain why a classification accuracy, a text likelihood, and a human preference score cannot be substituted for one another without a task definition. The modeling family, training objective, and evaluation each answer a different question.
