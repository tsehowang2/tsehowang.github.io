A score is not yet a probability

For the prefix “I go to school by,” imagine a model produces scores bus=2, train=1, bicycle=0. Scores can be any real numbers. They do not add to one, and they may be negative. A model needs a transformation if we want a next-token probability distribution.

Softmax converts a list of scores into positive numbers that sum to one. It exponentiates each score and divides by the sum of all exponentials. Bigger scores receive more probability, but the size of a score matters relative to the other candidates.

The term logit refers to the unnormalized model score in this setting. Do not interpret a logit of 2 as 200% or a probability of 0.2.

Softmax by hand

For [2,1,0], the exponentials are approximately [7.389,2.718,1]. Their sum is 11.107. The resulting probabilities are approximately [0.665,0.245,0.090].

Add 100 to every score. The probabilities remain the same because the common multiplicative factor cancels between numerator and denominator. An implementation subtracts the largest score before exponentiating to improve numerical stability.

For [1000,999], subtracting 1000 gives [0,-1]. The probabilities are approximately [0.731,0.269]. We can compute them without taking exp(1000), which would overflow ordinary floating-point arithmetic.

The expected token supplies a loss

Suppose the actual training sentence ends with train. Using its unrounded probability 0.244728, cross entropy is −ln(0.244728)≈1.408 nats. If training changes the model so train receives probability 0.7, the loss becomes −ln(0.7)≈0.357 nats.

The loss rewards assigning probability to the observed target. It does not say that every other transportation choice is impossible. Across many examples, the model balances different targets and contexts under its capacity and training procedure.

An overconfident wrong prediction can be expensive: target probability 0.01 gives loss about 4.605. This explains why accuracy alone does not describe the entire training objective. Two models may select the same most likely token while assigning quite different target probabilities.

Cross entropy, likelihood, and units

For observed targets y1,…,yN, maximum likelihood chooses parameters that make the product of their conditional probabilities large. Taking a natural logarithm turns that product into a sum. Minimizing the mean negative log likelihood is therefore the same optimization as maximizing likelihood. With a one-hot target at each position, this negative log likelihood is the cross-entropy loss used above.

For a target distribution p and prediction q, cross entropy is H(p,q)=−Σ p_i ln(q_i). It equals H(p)+KL(p||q). H(p) is fixed by the target distribution, so reducing cross entropy reduces the KL divergence from target to prediction. In the single one-hot example, H(p)=0 and the loss is simply −ln(q_target).

Natural logarithms measure loss in nats. Base-2 logarithms measure it in bits: loss_bits=loss_nats/ln(2). A uniform choice among four tokens gives −ln(1/4)=ln4≈1.386 nats, or exactly 2 bits. Keep the log base fixed when comparing reported losses.

Compute log probabilities without numerical overflow

Use log_softmax directly rather than taking log(softmax(logits)) as two separate numerical steps. For logits z, logsumexp(z)=m+ln Σ exp(z_i−m), where m is the largest logit. Then log_softmax(z)_i=z_i−logsumexp(z). This keeps large exponentials out of the calculation.

For [1000,999], logsumexp is approximately 1000.3133 and log probabilities are [−0.3133,−1.3133]. If the second token is correct, its loss is 1.3133 nats. PyTorch CrossEntropyLoss and functional.cross_entropy accept raw logits and class IDs; they already combine log_softmax with the negative log-likelihood calculation. Do not apply softmax first.

Where the scores come from

Let h be a contextual vector with d_model coordinates. A vocabulary output layer can compute z=hW+b, where W has shape [d_model,V] and V is vocabulary size. Each output coordinate is a score for one vocabulary entry.

If h has shape [1,4] and the vocabulary has ten entries, W has shape [4,10] and z has shape [1,10]. Softmax runs across those ten candidate scores. The hidden width and vocabulary size describe different axes.

We have not yet built the contextual vector h. The next several weeks will explain how a transformer constructs it from token and position information.

Gradients connect prediction to parameters

For softmax followed by cross entropy, the derivative with respect to a logit is predicted probability minus the target's one-hot indicator. If train is the target, the derivative vector for [0.665,0.245,0.090] is approximately [0.665,-0.755,0.090].

Gradient descent subtracts a scaled gradient. At the logit level, this points toward reducing the competing scores and increasing the target score. In a real network, the chain rule propagates the signal through the output layer and earlier representations.

This is a learning signal, not a hand-written language rule such as “after by always choose bus.” The same parameters participate in many prediction tasks, so learning aggregates evidence across them.

Temperature changes a distribution

Sampling sometimes uses softmax(z/temperature). With logits [2,0], temperature 1 gives about [0.881,0.119]. Temperature 2 gives about [0.731,0.269]. A larger temperature flattens this example's distribution.

Temperature is an inference choice here. It does not rewrite the training collection or verify a fact. Greedy decoding selects the largest logit directly; it should not be implemented by dividing by temperature zero.

We will study decoding more fully after building the transformer. For now, recognize that the same scores can lead to different sampling behavior under a different transformation.

Loss across several prediction positions

A sentence supplies a target at each valid next-token position. If three target probabilities are 0.5,0.25,0.25, the average negative log probability is (ln2+ln4+ln4)/3, approximately 1.155. Perplexity is exp(average loss), about 3.175.

Perplexity is exp(mean loss in nats), or 2^(mean loss in bits). A uniform four-token distribution has perplexity 4, so we can interpret it as an effective branching factor per prediction. For uneven predictions it is the geometric mean of inverse target probabilities, not a literal count of equally likely choices. Compare perplexity only under the same tokenization and evaluation protocol.

Padding positions should not become ordinary prediction targets. We will distinguish information masks and loss masks when we train a complete sequence model.

Worked practice and a readiness check

Compute softmax([0,0,0]). Each candidate has probability 1/3. If the correct token is bus, its cross entropy is ln3, about 1.099. Explain why subtracting a common constant from all three scores leaves the answer unchanged.

Then predict what happens if the bus score increases while the others stay fixed. Calculate the new distribution rather than stating only that bus “becomes more likely.”

You should be able to identify the score vector, the vocabulary axis, the target ID, the probability distribution, and the loss. The attention mechanism will reuse softmax for a different purpose: choosing mixture weights across available positions.

A second loss example with two students

Imagine two models both choose bus as their most probable token, while the true target is train. Model A assigns train probability 0.4, and model B assigns it 0.01. Both have zero top-one accuracy on this example, but their losses are about 0.916 and 4.605. The objective distinguishes uncertainty from an extremely confident mistake.

Now suppose the next example's target is bus. A complete comparison should include both examples rather than selecting only the one favoring a model. Average losses over the declared valid prediction positions.

This is why we keep the distribution and target visible when learning. A final selected word discards information that the optimizer uses. Later evaluation will also need more than one flattering example, although application metrics can differ from next-token loss.
