contrastive

Contrastive learning compares labeled matching pairs with alternatives in the same objective.

A simple school handbook

Imagine three handbook passages. H1 says “School opens at 08:00.” H2 says “The school bus leaves at 07:30.” H3 says “A ticket for the school bus costs 4 dollars.” A student asks “When does school start?”

Searching is the task of choosing useful passages for this question. The question uses start while H1 uses opens. Exact word matching can find school, but it may also find the bus passage. A good representation should help distinguish useful evidence from merely related text.

We postponed this application until after learning representations and transformers. Now “embed the question” refers to a concrete kind of numerical computation, not an unexplained magic API.

A lexical baseline first

A lexical retriever compares words or terms. An inverted index maps a term to passages containing it. Term weighting can reduce the influence of common words and emphasize more distinctive terms.

For the question about school starting, a simple overlap method may score both H1 and H2. The failure is understandable. The method lacks a learned relationship between start and opens, and it may not distinguish the main subject well.

BM25 is a widely used lexical scoring family with term-frequency saturation and length normalization. We will compare it with semantic methods rather than assuming neural vectors win on every kind of query. Exact API names and error codes often reward precise lexical matching.

A sentence embedding represents the input for a task

A transformer encoder can produce contextual token vectors. A pooling or other representation procedure creates a passage or sentence vector. Training then encourages useful geometry for the intended comparison.

An embedding model trained for retrieval aims to make relevant question-passage pairs score well under its chosen similarity function. A random average of token rows is a different method with different limitations.

The question and passage can be encoded independently in a bi-encoder. Passage vectors can be stored once and reused for many questions. This reuse is the computational reason that dense retrieval can scale beyond re-reading every passage jointly with every query.

A tiny numerical ranking

Use the invented unit question vector q=[1,0]. Let H1=[0.9,√0.19], H2=[0.6,0.8], and H3=[0,1]. All three passage vectors have unit length. Their dot products with q are exactly 0.9, 0.6, and 0. The second coordinate of H1 is about 0.43589.

H1 ranks first. These coordinates are designed for calculation; they are not measured outputs from a real embedding model. The ranking operation is exact given the vectors, while whether the vectors encode the right relevance remains empirical.

In the tutorial, replace invented coordinates with actual model embeddings. Keep the question set and evidence labels fixed so the experiment tests the representation rather than a hand-picked demonstration.

How retrieval embeddings can be trained

A positive pair is a question and a passage that supports its answer. A negative pair does not support it. A contrastive objective can increase the positive score relative to competing scores.

A hard negative resembles the question but is wrong. For “When does school open?” the bus departure time is a useful distractor. A passage about school opening in a different year is another.

False negatives occur when a passage marked negative actually provides useful evidence. Dataset construction therefore matters. The embedding space reflects the objective and labels, not an objective universal measure of meaning.

A contrastive training example with three questions

Return to our school handbook. Q1 is “When does school start?”, Q2 is “When does the school bus leave?”, and Q3 is “How much does a ticket for the school bus cost?” Their supporting passages are H1, H2, and H3 respectively. We label these positive pairs before training. The other passages in this small example do not answer that particular question.

A bi-encoder encodes each question and each passage separately. The following table contains invented training logits, after any similarity scaling, so we can calculate the objective easily. They are not raw cosine similarities: a cosine cannot equal 2. Larger logits give a pair more probability relative to the other candidates.

Question H1 H2 H3
Q1 2 1 0
Q2 0 2 1
Q3 1 0 2

For Q1, the positive passage is H1. Apply the same softmax operation you learned in week 4 to the row [2,1,0]. With temperature equal to 1 for this calculation, the normalized candidate probabilities are approximately [0.66524,0.24473,0.09003]. These probabilities describe the training choice among these three candidates; they are not calibrated probabilities that a passage is true or sufficient.

The loss for Q1 is the negative natural logarithm of the probability assigned to H1: −ln(0.66524), approximately 0.40761. Q2 and Q3 have the same score pattern with their positive in a different column, so the mean loss over these three rows is also approximately 0.40761. We want the positive to score well relative to competing passages, not merely to make every similarity score larger.

To see the learning direction, the derivative of this row loss with respect to its three scores is approximately [−0.33476,0.24473,0.09003] at temperature 1. Gradient descent therefore pushes the positive score upward and competing scores downward locally. In a real model, backpropagation follows the score through the question and passage encoders to update their trainable parameters. We do not keep this small score table as the model.

Temperature connects cosine similarity to training logits

The earlier [2,1,0] row is a teaching example with temperature 1. In a common retrieval setup, normalize embeddings, compute cosine similarities, then divide by a temperature τ: logits = cosine/τ. Equivalently, multiply cosine by scale=1/τ. The model and objective determine the appropriate scale; training libraries may fix or learn it.

For invented cosines [0.8,0.6,0.2] and τ=0.05, logits are [16,12,4]. Stable softmax gives positive-candidate probability about 0.982008 and positive loss about 0.018156. At τ=1, the same cosines give probability about 0.422379 and loss about 0.861852. Temperature changes the sharpness and gradient scale, so a loss near 0.41 from our earlier example is not a universal practical target.

A false negative changes what the model is taught

Now replace H2 with “Classes at this school begin at 08:00.” For Q1, this is another useful answer, not a negative. Keeping only H1 as the positive would penalize the model for also matching H2. If the Q1 scores change from [2,1,0] to [2,2,0], that single-positive loss rises to about 0.75862 even though the second supporting passage now scores better. The problem is the label assumption. Review duplicates and passages with multiple valid answers before treating other batch items as negatives.

Bi-encoder retrieval and cross-encoder reranking

The bi-encoder stores passage vectors ahead of time and scores a new question against them. A cross-encoder instead reads a question and a candidate passage together and produces a pair score. It can examine their token interactions directly, but must process each selected pair. A common arrangement retrieves a small candidate set with a bi-encoder, then reranks those candidates with a cross-encoder [ Sentence Transformers retrieve and rerank (R28) ]. A reranker cannot recover a supporting passage that was never included in its candidate set.

Check your understanding: identify the positive column in each table row; reproduce the Q1 softmax and loss; explain why another correct school-opening passage must not automatically become a negative. This is one common contrastive setup [ Sentence Transformers contrastive losses (R27) ], not the only way to train an embedding model.

Prepare the query and passage the way the model expects

An embedding checkpoint includes an input recipe. Some retrieval models distinguish inputs with prefixes such as “query: When does school start?” and “passage: School opens at 08:00.” Other models use an instruction template or no prefix. Follow that model card and its query/document encoding methods, including pooling, normalization, maximum length, and truncation. Adding an arbitrary prefix to every model is not an improvement.

One vector, several vectors, or sparse term weights

A Matryoshka-trained embedding organizes useful information into nested prefixes of its vector. A model trained for dimensions 64, 128, and 256 can let you choose a supported prefix length, renormalize if required, and compare the quality/storage tradeoff. Ordinary embeddings have no such guarantee: cutting any 256-dimensional vector to its first 64 numbers can discard essential information.

ColBERT keeps multiple contextual token vectors for a passage rather than pooling everything into one vector. In its late-interaction score, each query token finds its strongest passage-token match, then those maxima are summed. For query tokens “bus fare”, suppose one passage gives maximum similarities [0.9,0.2] and another [0.7,0.8]. Their illustrative scores are 1.1 and 1.5; the second covers both concepts better. Passage representations can be precomputed, but many token vectors need more index storage than one vector per passage.

SPLADE takes another route: a neural encoder produces a sparse vector over vocabulary terms, with learned term expansion. A query containing “bus fare” may receive weight on “ticket” even when that word was not typed. An inverted index can compare the nonzero term weights. This differs from BM25’s hand-designed term weighting and from a compact dense vector. The choices address different matching and storage requirements; evaluate them on the same questions.

Relevance needs labels and a definition

For our question, H1 is relevant. For “How much does the school bus cost and when does it leave?” both H2 and H3 are necessary. A single related passage may be insufficient for a complete task.

Hit@k asks whether at least one relevant passage appears. Recall@k asks what fraction of required relevant passages appear. We will compute both carefully in week 21.

An unsupported question, such as “What color is the headteacher's car?”, has no supporting passage in this small handbook. A retriever can still return a nearest vector. Nearest does not mean sufficient.

Moving toward technical documentation

The same structure applies when passages describe API parameters instead of school schedules. Exact names, version constraints, and code blocks become important. The approachable handbook example teaches the mechanism first; the documentation corpus supplies the harder setting.

Choose a fixed corpus revision and retain passage IDs and source provenance. Keep question labels separate from training or configuration tuning. Do not select evaluation questions only after seeing which ones the new retriever handles well.

We will add persistence, metadata, and approximate search next week. You can understand and test the ranking mechanism with a list of vectors before operating a database.

Reference details and checks

Review A: Intuition

A retriever must rank useful evidence, not merely related text. Exact API names and error codes favor lexical matching; paraphrased intent may favor semantic matching. Start with both kinds of questions and a simple baseline. Choose a scoring method because it addresses an observed failure, not because it has “vector” in its name.

An inverted index maps terms to matching documents. TF-IDF weights frequent-in-document but rare-in-corpus terms. BM25 additionally uses term-frequency saturation and document-length normalization. Dense retrieval encodes queries and passages into a shared vector space and compares them. A lexical baseline provides a valuable control for dense retrieval, especially on technical symbols.

Review A: Relevance and metrics

Let G be the set of relevant evidence IDs and Rk the first k returned IDs. Recall@k is |G intersection Rk| / |G| . Hit@k is one if any relevant item appears. Reciprocal rank is 1/r for the first relevant rank r, zero if none appears. When more than one passage is relevant, Hit@k and Recall@k answer different questions. NDCG can represent graded relevance and position discounts, provided the relevance labels and normalization are defined.

Review A: Worked example

If G={A,B} and the first three results are C,A,D, Hit@3=1, Recall@3=1/2 and reciprocal rank=1/2. A report calling Hit@3 “recall” hides the missing second piece of evidence. For a comparison task requiring both versions, that missing piece may make the answer impossible.

For unanswerable questions with no relevant evidence, the ordinary recall denominator is zero. Do not silently assign perfect recall. Evaluate those questions separately through refusal, false-positive retrieval or unsupported-answer measures. The starter represents their retrieval metrics as not applicable.

Review A: Experiment

Tutorial 18: A vector database from a list of vectors compares the bundled lexical retriever with actual sentence-transformer embeddings. Keep document chunks, questions, version filtering and k fixed. Include exact identifiers, paraphrases and wrong-version distractors. Save per-query ranks before computing averages; a mean can hide a systematic class of failures.

Separate model failure from ingestion failure. Inspect whether the expected passage actually exists in the index, whether truncation removed its key sentence, and whether metadata filters excluded it. For the real capstone, label enough question types that one example cannot dominate a slice. The tiny teaching fixture is a software correctness aid, not a sufficient research dataset.

Exit criterion: calculate recall, hit rate and reciprocal rank by hand, then explain a dense-versus-lexical tradeoff using a real failure. Reading: Sentence Transformers semantic similarity (R07) and Robertson and Zaragoza Probabilistic Relevance Framework (R25) concepts. Tutorial: 17.
