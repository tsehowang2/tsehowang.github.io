Why the model needs a representation

We can read “I go to school by bus,” but a numerical model needs a reproducible way to represent it. Start with the toy rule “split at spaces.” The sentence becomes six tokens: I, go, to, school, by, bus. A vocabulary lists the token types the model knows.

Assign IDs: I=0, go=1, to=2, school=3, by=4, bus=5. The sentence becomes [0,1,2,3,4,5]. An ID is a label. School does not become more meaningful because its ID is larger than the ID for go. If we consistently permuted the IDs and corresponding model tables, the labels themselves would not change the task.

Keep three objects separate: the original text, the token sequence, and the ID sequence. A tokenizer determines the conversion. A vocabulary determines which IDs correspond to which token strings. The model consumes those IDs through a representation layer.

Punctuation reveals a weakness in word splitting

Compare “bus” and “bus.” A naive space splitter treats them as different strings. What about “school-bus,” “school bus,” or a misspelling? Treating every possible word form as a separate vocabulary item creates many rare entries and difficulty with unseen words.

A character tokenizer instead represents b, u, s, and punctuation separately. It has a smaller basic inventory but creates longer sequences. A subword tokenizer uses reusable pieces between whole words and characters. Common words may be single tokens while less common words break into several pieces.

There is no rule that one token equals one word. Once we use a real model, we must inspect its actual tokenizer. Word splitting remains useful as a teaching device because we can follow every ID by hand.

A vocabulary is learned or chosen before use

Suppose the vocabulary includes school and schools as whole tokens but not schoolyard. A word-level system needs an unknown-token policy. A subword system might encode schoolyard using school and yard, or other pieces depending on the learned vocabulary.

Tokenization must stay compatible with the trained model. If ID 5 meant bus during training and means train during inference, the model receives the wrong representation even though both inputs are valid integers. A model checkpoint and its tokenizer therefore belong together.

Special tokens may mark the beginning or end of a sequence, padding, or conversation boundaries. Their exact meanings depend on the model. We will introduce conversation templates later rather than assuming that a visible word such as “assistant” automatically acts as a special role marker.

Byte pair encoding with a tiny example

Use the invented training corpus bus four times, busy twice, and plus once. Split each word into characters and a visible end marker _. We count adjacent pairs with the word frequencies included: u,s occurs 7 times, b,u occurs 6 times, and s,_ occurs 5 times. The other pairs occur less often. Therefore u,s is the unique most frequent pair and becomes us.

Word and frequency Before the first merge After u,s becomes us
bus × 4 b u s _ b us _
busy × 2 b u s y _ b us y _
plus × 1 p l u s _ p l us _

Count again after the merge. The pair b,us occurs 6 times, while us,_ occurs 5 times. The next merge is b,us into bus. BPE repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair in the current representation; it does not choose the longest word or permanently keep the original pair counts. Ties in other corpora require a deterministic rule.

Save the learned vocabulary and merge order. Encoding a new sentence uses those saved rules; fitting a fresh tokenizer for every sentence would change the meaning of the model input IDs. The end marker in this hand calculation is a teaching convention, not a claim about a particular pretrained tokenizer.

Bytes, subword algorithms, and SentencePiece

Byte-level BPE starts from byte symbols rather than a separate initial symbol for every Unicode character. The English word bus is the UTF-8 bytes [98,117,115]. A Chinese character can occupy several UTF-8 bytes; learned merges may combine those bytes into larger tokens. A complete byte alphabet can represent any UTF-8 input, although normalization and decoding settings still determine the final text round trip. Byte count and model token count are different measurements.

BPE learns frequent pair merges. WordPiece is another subword family: its vocabulary-training criterion differs from simple BPE frequency counting, and common implementations encode words by matching vocabulary pieces. Unigram begins with candidate pieces and learns a probabilistic segmentation model while pruning the vocabulary. SentencePiece is a tokenizer toolkit and text-processing approach that can train BPE or Unigram models directly from raw text; it is not a fourth mutually exclusive merge algorithm.

Whitespace and digits are part of the input. The strings bus and “ bus” can follow different tokenization paths, and 2026 may split into one or several pieces depending on the tokenizer. Visible markers such as a leading-space symbol are tokenizer conventions. Inspect token IDs and decoded pieces rather than removing spaces or assuming that every number is a single token.

English and Traditional Chinese use different token budgets

For a Hong Kong application, compare “I go to school by bus.” with a meaning-matched Traditional Chinese sentence such as “我搭巴士返學。” using the exact tokenizer of the chosen model. Record text, UTF-8 byte count, token IDs, number of tokens, and the tokenizer revision. Repeat with a code identifier, a date, and English mixed with Chinese. This separates language coverage from sentence length and formatting.

Tokenizers allocate vocabulary capacity differently, so there is no fixed English-to-Chinese token ratio. Some English-heavy vocabularies encode Chinese less compactly; a multilingual vocabulary may behave differently. More tokens use more context space and can increase model work and token-priced service cost. Translate first, measure with the deployed tokenizer, and budget input and output tokens separately.

IDs become vectors through a lookup table

An embedding table contains one learned vector per vocabulary entry. If the table has six rows and each vector has three coordinates, its shape is [6,3]. Looking up bus, ID 5, selects row 5.

For illustration, assign bus the vector [0.2,0.8,0.1] and school [0.9,0.1,0.4]. These coordinates are invented numbers for tracing the computation. They are not a statement that the first coordinate literally means education or the second means transport.

The six-token sentence now becomes a matrix with six rows and three columns. Each row corresponds to one position in the sequence. Week 3 will examine vector geometry; attention will later combine information across these rows.

One hot encoding and lookup mean the same selection

A one-hot vector for ID 5 has six coordinates, all zero except a one at index 5. Multiplying that row vector by the [6,3] embedding table selects the same row as a lookup. This equivalence explains the operation mathematically.

An implementation normally performs an efficient indexing operation instead of materializing a huge one-hot matrix. The distinction is between a mathematical description and an efficient implementation of the same selection.

At this point, the vector for bus does not depend on its sentence. The lookup produces the same starting row wherever bus appears. Contextual processing will change the representation using surrounding tokens.

Length and decoding

If a word tokenizer produces six tokens but a character tokenizer produces twenty-one, a model processes sequences of different lengths for the same text. Length influences how much computation and memory later stages need.

Decoding converts IDs back to text under the tokenizer's rules. Whitespace and special tokens deserve attention; joining token strings with spaces is not a universal decoding algorithm. Keep the original string when checking round-trip behavior.

Our tutorial compares a simple word tokenizer with UTF-8 bytes. Byte count is useful to inspect encoding, but it is not a substitute for the token count of a pretrained model.

Worked practice and a readiness check

Write a vocabulary for “I go to school by bus” and “You go to work by train.” Decide how you will represent a period and an end marker. Encode both sentences and decode them again. Then encode “I go to school by bicycle” when bicycle is absent.

State your policy for the unknown token, and explain why another tokenizer could avoid that particular unknown by using smaller pieces. Given a vocabulary of 100 entries and vector width 16, calculate 1600 embedding parameters. Given a sentence of seven tokens, write its representation shape as [7,16].

You are ready when you can explain why IDs are labels, why the tokenizer must match the model, and how an ID selects a vector. The lookup itself is an indexing operation on the embedding table.
