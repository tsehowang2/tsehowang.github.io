Completing text and following a request

A pretrained language model learns patterns from prediction tasks. A request such as “Complete this sentence with one transport word” asks for a particular style of behavior.

The model may have acquired some useful behavior during pretraining, but supervised examples can make desired formats and responses more consistent. We distinguish the model's general prediction training from later adaptation.

Keep using simple tasks. Given “I go to school by ___,” one desired response is bus. Given “Name two possible completions,” a desired response could be bus and train. The task instruction changes what a satisfactory response looks like.

Pretraining learns from broad text

Next-token pretraining uses text to create many shifted prediction targets. The data and objective do not guarantee that every generated claim will be correct.

The model learns parameters that support predictions across its training distribution. It does not store every sentence as an ordinary searchable row, although memorization can occur.

An embedding table, attention projections, MLP weights, and output head all participate in this parameterized computation. The full path from the earlier lectures remains present after training.

Supervised fine tuning supplies desired responses

An SFT example pairs an input with a target output. For a single-turn teaching example, the input might contain an instruction and sentence prefix, and the target might be the desired completion.

Training may calculate loss only on assistant-response tokens while keeping prompt tokens available as context. This is loss masking, separate from causal attention masking.

This week establishes the idea. We will construct datasets, adapters, and controlled fine-tuning experiments in weeks 24–26 after studying evaluation and compute resources.

Preferences compare candidate responses

A preference example contains a prompt and two possible responses, with one preferred under a stated rubric. A correct, concise completion can be preferred to an unrelated answer.

RLHF often uses preference-derived reward information in a reinforcement-learning procedure. DPO uses a direct preference objective relative to a reference model. The details will come later; both require thoughtful data and evaluation.

Preference is a signal about judged behavior. A reward for pleasant wording alone can favor fluent but incorrect responses. The rubric matters as much as the algorithm's name.

Reasoning as intermediate computation

Consider “A bus ticket costs 4 dollars. I buy three tickets. What is the total?” The checkable result is 12 dollars. A response may show the concise calculation 3×4=12.

Chain-of-thought prompting uses intermediate steps or examples to help some models with some tasks. A generated rationale can still accompany a wrong final answer. It is not guaranteed to reveal the model's actual internal causal process.

Reasoning-trained models may use extra inference computation. We should evaluate final correctness and resource use rather than judging ability by the length of a displayed explanation.

A multi-step answer can fail at a specific boundary

Use this problem: “A school buys 8 adult tickets at 6 dollars and 12 student tickets at 4 dollars. A 25% discount applies only to the student tickets. What is the total?” The adult subtotal is 8×6=48. The student subtotal is 12×4=48, discounted to 36. The correct total is 84. A fluent but wrong solution discounts the whole 96-dollar bill and returns 72. The failure is the scope of “only”, not multiplication.

Compare a direct answer with a prompt asking for adult subtotal, eligible student subtotal, discount, and final total. Record the final correctness and whether the discount was applied to the correct group. Do not claim this prompt helped until the same comparison has been run over several independently checked problems. A displayed sequence of steps makes an error easier for a reader to inspect; it is not proof that the sequence faithfully describes all internal computation.

Test-time compute spends work after training

A fixed model can spend more inference work by generating a longer intermediate computation, sampling several candidates, searching alternatives, or using a verifier. For the ticket problem, sample three candidates and check the two subtotals and discount scope. Report all generated tokens and verification calls, including rejected candidates. More test-time compute buys opportunities to find or check a solution; the selection rule determines whether those opportunities improve the final answer.

PPO, GRPO, RLVR, and constitutional feedback

In reinforcement-learning post-training, the language model is a policy that assigns probabilities to response tokens. A reward scores a sampled response. PPO compares new and old policy probabilities and clips the training objective to discourage overly large updates; common RLHF setups also control drift from a reference model. Its value estimator helps estimate how much better a response was than expected. This is parameter training, unlike asking a fixed model to show steps.

GRPO samples a group of responses for the same problem and compares their rewards within the group. A typical form subtracts the group mean and divides by a stabilized group standard deviation to form relative advantages, avoiding a separate learned value model in that setup. If every candidate receives the same reward, the group offers little or no relative learning signal. Implementations differ in normalization, clipping, and reference-policy penalties, so identify the actual objective used.

RLVR means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. A checker might test the final ticket total, run a unit test, or verify a mathematical result. It supplies a more directly checkable signal than “sounds convincing”, but a weak checker can still reward shortcuts. Checking only whether the final string is “84” does not prove that every displayed step is correct.

Constitutional methods use written principles to guide critique, revision, or preference judgments. For example, ask a model to critique an answer against a principle requiring evidence-based claims, then revise it; AI feedback may also help form training preferences. The principles and reviewer become part of the learning process and require evaluation for bias and errors. Constitutional feedback, human preferences, verifiable rewards, and SFT labels are different sources of supervision that a training recipe can combine.

A fair small reasoning experiment

Prepare arithmetic examples with independent answers. Compare direct responses with concise step-based prompts using the same model, task set, and decoding settings where appropriate.

Count all generated candidates and verification calls. If one method samples five answers before selecting one, its cost includes all five.

For a simple sentence completion, extra reasoning may add no value. For arithmetic, a calculator can provide an independently checked result. We will introduce executable tools only after the underlying model and later application task are clear.

In context learning and weight changes

Providing examples in the prompt can influence the current output without changing model weights. Fine-tuning changes trainable parameters using an optimizer.

Suppose the prompt gives two examples of answering with a single word. That is contextual instruction or demonstration. Saving an adapter after gradient updates is a different intervention.

This distinction will matter when diagnosing whether a failure needs a better prompt, better evidence, or a learned change. For now, identify which information lives in the input and which lives in parameters.

Worked practice and a readiness check

Write two SFT examples for sentence completion and one preference pair where the preferred output follows a one-word instruction. Identify prompt tokens and answer tokens for the loss.

For the ticket problem, write the correct answer and an attractive but incorrect explanation. Explain why scoring only explanation fluency would fail.

You are ready when pretraining, SFT, preferences, in-context examples, and reasoning prompts have different meanings in your explanation. We will return to their harder objectives and experiments in semester two.

A response can follow format and still be wrong

The instruction asks for one word completing “I go to school by ___.” A target bus teaches a format as well as an association. If every example insists on bus regardless of evidence, the model can learn a narrow pattern that fails when the context says the speaker walks.

Use a contrasting example: “The bus service is closed today. I go to school by ___.” The desired completion may now depend on additional information, and several answers may remain plausible. A dataset that pretends there is always one uniquely correct answer can encode unwarranted certainty.

For instruction-following evaluations, separate compliance with “one word” from whether the content fits the supplied facts. For reasoning, separate a correct final amount from a convincing-looking calculation. These distinctions will guide our later adaptation experiments and prevent a lower loss from becoming an automatic claim of better behavior.
