Semester 1 / Week 03

What word vectors can represent

Tutorial 03Test your understanding
Lecture slides · Week 03

Learn one step at a time.

Read each explanation, pause to predict the result, then continue. Use the arrow keys while the presentation is focused. The complete lecture reader is below.

Start with an example

Two words can be different labels but related inputs

Our count model treated “I go to school by” and “I go to work by” as unrelated exact strings. A neural model can reuse numerical features across examples. If school and work occur in some similar travel contexts, their learned representations may share useful properties for predicting transportation words.

This does not mean the two words have identical meaning. A representation is useful for a task when its coordinates help the model reduce that task's error. What the model learns depends on the data and objective.

Start with an ordinary two-dimensional vector so we can draw it. A vector has a direction and a length. Those two properties affect common similarity calculations differently.

Start with an example

A dot product is a weighted comparison

For vectors q=[1,0] and a=[0.8,0.6], the dot product is 1×0.8+0×0.6=0.8. For b=[4,3], it is 4. The larger answer for b reflects both direction and magnitude.

The dot product can be understood as how strongly two coordinate patterns align. Negative and positive coordinates can contribute in opposite directions. It is a numerical operation, not an automatic definition of semantic similarity.

Later, attention will use dot products between learned query and key vectors. We study the operation now so that the attention equation will not introduce geometry and model architecture in the same step.

Build the mechanism

Cosine compares direction

The length of a is sqrt(0.8²+0.6²)=1. The length of b is sqrt(4²+3²)=5. Dividing the dot product by the product of lengths gives cosine similarity. Both vectors have cosine 0.8 with q.

That makes sense geometrically: b is five times a, so they point in the same direction. Normalizing a nonzero vector to length one removes its magnitude. After normalization, their dot products with a unit q are equal.

Neither score is universally correct. A model may use magnitude as part of its learned computation. For a particular retrieval task, direction-only scoring may be useful. Choose the operation that matches the training objective and the system you are studying.

Build the mechanism

An embedding table learns through prediction errors

Suppose a simple model sees the token school and predicts the next token. It selects the school row, transforms it, and produces scores over vocabulary entries. If the correct next token is by and the model gives it too little probability, training changes parameters through gradients.

The embedding coordinates are among the parameters that can change. Repeated contexts create pressure for useful shared structure. We are not manually assigning school an “education coordinate.”

Different random seeds can produce different coordinate values that support similar predictions. Rotating a representation space together with compatible downstream transformations can preserve relevant behavior. This is one reason to avoid interpreting individual coordinates as fixed human concepts.

Build the mechanism

Starting vectors and contextual vectors

The starting vector for bus comes from its vocabulary row. In “I go to school by bus” and “The bus broke down,” that lookup row can be the same. After context processing, the representation at bus can differ between the sentences.

The context tells us whether we are discussing a travel choice or a breakdown. A contextual vector represents a token at a position under available context. Later, the causal mask will determine which other positions are available.

Keep token embeddings, contextual token representations, and whole-sentence embeddings distinct. A vector chosen from a lookup table is not automatically a good summary of an entire paragraph.

Build the mechanism

A simple sentence representation and its limitations

One easy way to represent a sentence is to average its token vectors. This combines information but loses much of the ordering. “The dog chased the boy” and “The boy chased the dog” contain the same word inventory under a simple tokenizer, so an order-insensitive average can be identical.

That limitation motivates context and position processing. Averaging can still be a useful baseline, but knowing its failure mode prevents us from mistaking convenience for a complete language model.

In week 17, we will return to sentence embeddings trained for search. That application requires an objective and evaluation of its own. For now, the goal is understanding how numerical representations can share information.

Go deeper and check understanding

A small numerical experiment

Move q from [1,0] toward [0,1]. At [0,1], its dot products with a and b become 0.6 and 3, while their cosines remain equal at 0.6. Normalizing the passage vectors changes the raw dot-product comparison.

Now try q=[-1,0]. The dot products become -0.8 and -4. Similarity conventions matter when scores can be negative. A maximum score still means “largest under this operation,” not “true with this probability.”

Go deeper and check understanding

Worked practice and a readiness check

Calculate the norm of [3,4], normalize it, and compute its cosine with [1,0]. The answers are 5, [0.6,0.8], and 0.6. Then compare [6,8] and explain what changes and what remains the same.

Explain why changing the embedding table without adjusting the rest of a trained model can change behavior. Explain why two separately trained 384-dimensional models do not necessarily use compatible coordinates.

You should now be able to follow an embedding lookup, a dot product, normalization, and the difference between an initial and contextual representation. These are the numerical building blocks we need before attention.

Go deeper and check understanding

A second representation example

Suppose a toy vocabulary has school=[1,0], work=[0.8,0.2], and bus=[0,1]. A mean representation for “school bus” is [0.5,0.5], while “work bus” gives [0.4,0.6]. Their similarity partly reflects a shared bus component. These coordinates are deliberately chosen; an actual model would need training and evaluation.

Now reverse “school bus” to “bus school.” The mean stays the same because addition is commutative. This gives an exact counterexample to the claim that averaging always preserves language meaning. We do not need a large benchmark to establish this particular invariance.

The next question is constructive: how could the model distinguish the orders? Position information can change the starting rows, and contextual processing can combine them differently. This connects the limitation of a simple vector average directly to the architecture we are building.
